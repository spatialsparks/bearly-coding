bearly-coding gives a project a board of sprints and stories, a small team of agents that builds them, and skills written for the stack you chose. Nothing is pushed before it has been reviewed.

Why it exists

Built for my own work first.

I built bearly-coding to help me with my own work. It is the workflow I have coded a variety of different apps with, and it grew with each of them before it became a plugin of its own.

There are great libraries of agent skills already, and Matt Pocock's skills are among the best. They did not fit my style, though. So I kept my own workflow, took the concepts of his that I found most interesting, and worked them into mine.

From an idea to a first sprint

Four commands, each in a fresh session.

The project carries everything it needs: the commands, the agents, the board, the documents and the git hooks are written into it, and yours to change. When you are unsure what comes next, /standup says.

Watch a sprint on autopilot

Five stories, two waves, one review, then it lands.

A sprint is one outcome you asked for. A sprint lead builds it in waves: the ready stories that are provably independent, one implementer each, in its own git worktree. Step through it, or switch to the run where a story needs you.

      Two ways to build, and no mode to set

      The command you type says what happens.

      The plain name is the careful one. The two never run at once: /implement stops while a wave is being built, and /autopilot stops when your working tree holds uncommitted changes.

      By hand

      /implement

      Youpick a story, read the diff, commit
      This sessiontest first, in your checkout
      • Builds one story, test first, inside its owned paths, both verifies green.
      • Stops and asks before a new dependency, a changed contract, or a choice between two designs.
      • Changes nothing in git: you read the diff, you commit, you push.
      • Given a whole sprint, it still builds one story at a time and waits for your word.
      By itself, for days if need be

      /autopilot

      Your sessionthe orchestrator: builds nothing, keeps a few lines per sprint
      Sprint leada fresh context for every sprint
      Implementersone per story, each in its own worktree
      Verifieronce per wave
      Two reviewersspec and standards, once per sprint
      • Never stops to ask: what needs you becomes a file, and everything that does not depend on it goes on.
      • A sprint is merged into main, pushed and deployed only after both reviewers have read it whole.
      • Stops at once only where going on could do harm: a secret, or anything that would become public.
      • To turn it off, stop the session. Nothing is scheduled.

      What waits on you

      Nobody stops to ask. You answer when you are back.

      Whenever building needs something only you can give, it writes one file, written for you coming back days later, and carries on with the rest. /resolve takes you through the open ones, the one that holds up the most first. Tap a card.

      Skills for your stack, written by your project

      No library to install, and none to outgrow.

      /kickoff starts one skill writer for each part of your stack. Each reads that part's own documentation for the version you use and writes one skill into .claude/skills/. Every rule names the page it came from; what a writer could not confirm is listed as open instead of guessed.

      Every command

      A command for every step, so nothing is pasted.

      Two come with the plugin. The rest are written into each project, so they have no prefix and you can change them there. Every command that plans is safe while a sprint is being built.

      What it holds to

      Rules that are checked, not hoped for.

      Private by default

      A new repository is created private. Nothing becomes public without your yes.

      Secrets stay out of git

      TruffleHog checks every commit and every push, and both are refused when it is missing.

      Reviewed before it leaves

      A sprint stays on your machine until two reviewers have read it as a whole.

      Every check runs locally

      No CI to pay for and no pull requests to shepherd, unless your project decides otherwise.

      No guessed permissions

      On autopilot, what the project's settings do not allow is refused, and costs one story, not the run.
